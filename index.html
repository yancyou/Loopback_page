01 Overview

Performance overview

At the same training tokens and executed depth, Loopback reaches lower validation loss than the standard Transformer from 191M to 3B, with 40–75% of its Transformer parameters.

02 Abstract

Abstract

Looped Transformers reduce parameter count by repeatedly applying shared blocks, but parameter sharing alone does not determine how the results of repeated computation are reused. We take the depth-memory view of residual connections and analyze which representation is stored after each block application and how later reads weight it. A full block output contains both its input and its new update, so weighting that output also reweights the history already carried by the input. Reading individual updates instead, with the shared stack’s input kept on a separate path, gives separate controls over input strength, total update weight, and allocation across updates. The Loopback Transformer implements these controls: geometric decay sets the total update weight and supplies a recency prior, and attention learns how to allocate that weight. On ClimbMix, Loopback reaches lower validation loss than standard Transformer baselines with 40–75% of their Transformer parameters (excluding token embeddings and the output head) at reference scales from 191M to 3B, with training tokens and executed depth matched. It also has higher mean accuracy over thirteen downstream tasks at 1.4B and 3B. At 191M, 374M, and 778M, it has the lowest validation loss and the highest thirteen-task mean among the standard Transformer and seven looped residual designs.

03 Contributions

Key contributions

1

Residual designs as depth memory

Linear attention and related recurrent models supply memory-update rules; adapted from token sequences to network depth, they describe what each block writes and how its contribution is transported and read. The view exposes two couplings: weighting a full block output also reweights the input it carries, and a joint softmax makes the input and the block updates compete for a fixed total weight.

2

Store updates, keep the input on its own path

Loopback adds a weighted sum of block updates to the original loop input. It stores each block’s update rather than its output, so the input is never written back, and it reads the loop input at coefficient one, so a softmax never shrinks it.

3

Decay sets the total, attention allocates it

Geometric decay keeps the total update weight bounded as loop iterations increase at a fixed stack size: a factor λ at the end of every iteration sets a recency prior whose total stays below Kλ/(1−λ). Attention adjusts each update’s weight by its content while preserving that total; zero queries give the decay-only loop.

4

Fewer parameters, lower loss

On ClimbMix, Loopback reaches lower validation loss and higher zero-shot task means than matched-depth standard Transformers at five scales from 191M to 3B, with 40–75% of their Transformer parameters, and the lowest loss and highest thirteen-task mean of nine designs at 191M, 374M and 778M.

04 Method

From residual addition to a controllable read

Three moves separate the controls that ordinary addition ties together: what is stored, how the input is carried, and how the stored weight is set and allocated.

  1. One interface for residual rules

    Addition, Attention Residuals, Hyper-Connections, mHC, Hyperloop and recurrent depth readers all write a value, carry old memory forward, and read it to form the next hidden state. Unrolling the interface gives every earlier update an explicit coefficient, which exposes two couplings: storing a full block output also rewrites the input it contains, and a joint softmax over the input and the updates ties their weights to a single unit.

  2. Store updates, keep the input on its own path, set the total with decay

    Loopback stores each block's update δ rather than its output and keeps the loop input at coefficient one. At the end of every loop iteration a geometric factor λ shrinks all stored weights, so the prior favors recent iterations and the total update weight stays bounded by Kλ/(1−λ).

  3. Attention allocates the total

    A destination query scores the normalized block updates and redistributes the decay-set total across them by content. Zero queries recover the decay-only loop exactly; λ = 1 with zero queries recovers ordinary addition. A fused Triton kernel streams the stored updates once per read.

06 Results

Results

Scale names give the standard Transformer’s total parameters, and every model trains on ClimbMix at 20 tokens per parameter of that count (20 TPP) under a warmup–stable–decay schedule. Every comparison within a scale matches the training tokens and the executed depth: the looped arms share one topology, and the standard Transformer keeps the same number of block applications. Validation loss is next-token cross-entropy on a held-out ClimbMix shard packed as in training. Downstream accuracy is zero-shot on thirteen tasks with the lm-evaluation-harness, every prompt starting with the beginning-of-sequence token as in training, plus WikiText word perplexity. Transformer parameters exclude embeddings and the vocabulary head; shared weights are counted once.

1.4B and 3B

At 1.4B and 3B, Loopback uses [3 → 8×2 → 6] and [6 → 6×2 → 6], matching the standard Transformer’s 25 and 24 executed layers with 68% and 75% of its Transformer parameters. The same 20-TPP checkpoints are evaluated on general benchmarks and on mathematical and code tasks; tests of additional loop iterations also include 191M, 374M and 1.4B models for comparison with Plain Loop.

Code and mathematics

Code and mathematical evaluation

Reference-solution loss on GSM8K, MATH-500, HumanEval and MBPP measures how well a model predicts a worked solution given the problem, and five-shot numeric-answer accuracy on six word-problem sets checks final answers. At matched training tokens and executed depth, Loopback has lower solution loss on all four datasets at both large scales, and at 3B its word-problem accuracy rises from 2.6% to 6.4%. In the nine-design comparisons at 191M and 374M, Loopback also has the lowest four-set mean.

Extra visits

Robustness to additional visits

Shared weights allow extra visits at inference, but excessive recurrence can degrade predictions, a problem known as overthinking. Without retraining, and with the extra destinations reusing the last trained visit’s queries, Loopback’s loss rises less than Plain Loop’s at every tested count at 191M, 374M and 1.4B, up to twice the trained count. At eight visits the 191M loss rises by 0.060 for Loopback against 0.454 for Plain Loop; at four visits the 374M increases are 0.074 against 0.270, and the 1.4B increases are 0.028 against 0.248. At 3B, one extra iteration raises Loopback’s thirteen-task mean from 49.45% to 50.17%.

Depth

Depth at fixed parameters

At 374M and 778M, every configuration trains from scratch with the training tokens held fixed and total parameters approximately fixed while width is traded for depth from 16 to 64 layers; the loop visits stay fixed at two. The standard Transformer loses ground as it gets deeper and narrower: it is best at 24 layers and, at 64, is 0.076 worse at 374M and 0.035 worse at 778M. Among the looped rules, Loopback is lowest at nine of the ten points, and its best tested depth moves from 24 layers at 374M to 48 at 778M. At the sweep endpoints, its margin over the standard Transformer increases from 0.003 at 16 layers to 0.073 at 64 layers at 374M, and from 0.002 to 0.028 at 778M.

Depth-memory readers

Linear attention and recurrent memory as loop readers

Nine recurrent cores, Mesa and a LaCT-inspired reader are adapted to read the loop’s history at visit boundaries, leaving token attention unchanged. Each reads either cumulative visit states or whole-visit updates, and both interfaces initialize to Plain Loop. Reading updates gives the lower validation loss in all 22 family–scale comparisons, and every update reader improves on Plain Loop, but the best still trail Loopback by 0.0302 nats at 191M and 0.0285 at 374M, which favors updates over states as the memory value. Fixed-state recurrent cores keep one practical advantage for long loops: each new write is absorbed without rereading the history, in a state that does not grow with the visit count; whether optimized recurrent kernels turn this into lower memory traffic or higher throughput is untested.
